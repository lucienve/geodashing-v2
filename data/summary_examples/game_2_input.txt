GD002-AMWD is above the towns of Blackhawk and Central City, 2 old mining settlements in the foothills of the Rocky Mountains that have evolved into a mini-Las Vegas. After a detour to hike to the summit of Maryland Mountain, I returned to Blackhawk, drove past all the casinos, and then zig-zagged up steep residential roads to the summit of a hill above Central City. The roads didn’t seem wide enough to support 2-way traffic, so I was glad to not meet any vehicles traveling in the opposite direction. On the flat summit of the hill, I found the GD002-AMWD dashpoint 20m west of Mac Rock Road, behind a fence. There wasn’t much to see at the dashpoint, other than the small square structure that I had observed in the satellite photo, which appeared to be an old outhouse.

Thanks to Lucien for reviving the game and giving me a reason to get out of the house and back into the mountains.

(The photo was taken from the summit of Maryland Mountain, not from the dashpoint. The mountain on the left is Mt Blue Sky, formerly called Mt Evans.)

While a surface approach was not practical, I did get somewhat close to GD002-BOST. The dashpoint is approximately a half-mile east of George Inlet, near Ketchikan, Alaska. To get close to the dashpoint, we flew in a 1961 de Havilland DHC-2 Beaver. It's widely considered one of the most rugged and reliable utility aircraft ever built, and was powered by a classic Pratt & Whitney R-985 Wasp Junior radial engine.  The flight controls and gauges are all analog and mechanical, but it has been upgraded with a modern electronic navigation display, bringing GPS to the 1960s mechanical aviation.
En route, we passed the George Inlet Cannery, a historic salmon packing facility established in 1914 that operated extensively until its closure in the late 1950s. We also observed the George Inlet Lodge along the shore; originally built in the 1940s as a cannery bunkhouse, the structure was later elevated and transported 90 miles via log raft through the Inside Passage to its current site.
Luckily, we also got to see a pod of three orcas swimming along the inlet. During early May, orcas frequently move through these inside passageways to hunt the early runs of returning king salmon, making for an active feeding season.

It was a beautiful, sunny afternoon for a dashpoint hunt.   This one seemed to be my best bet this month - right off of a trail, with parking nearby.

Parking near the trailhead was straightforward; it's clearly well-used by hikers, and there was a handwritten note on the bulletin board thanking a local "trail angel" for the treats that had been left.. Since it's still a bit early in the year for the peak season of northbound thru-hikers, the woods were quiet, and I had the trail to myself.

I had actually hiked this part of the Appalachian Trail back in the 1990s, and it was a nice trip back to revisit it. This specific stretch in Warwick holds an interesting place in hiking history: a ten-miile portion of what would become the AT was blazed by the Boy Scouts and other local community groups in 1931, six years before the AT was officially dedicated and fully joined from Georgia to Maine.

I managed to get right up to the point of closest approach on the trail, but the final stretch to the dashpoint proved difficult. The undergrowth was thick with knee-high briars and thorns, and after testing a few different potential paths, I decided discretion was the better part of valor. I gave up on reaching the dashpoint (while sustaining only a minor amount of blood loss..). Instead, I did find a peaceful, wide-open old field out in the woods. It was a beautiful spot under a clear blue sky, and I spent a quiet moment just sitting there enjoying the silence and the sounds of nature.

On the drive home, I stopped into the town of Warwick. It's a neat little place with an active downtown and some beautiful 19th-century buildings. I picked up some dinner to take home, wrapping up a perfect afternoon out on the trail and some memories.
